# FindPhotosOfMe landing page creative brief

Prepared for Ivan and Claude Code on 2 October 2026. This is a copy library and creative direction for three local, reviewable landing page concepts. It is not a statement that the unfinished product is ready to sell.

## The product and the main decision

FindPhotosOfMe helps people find photographs of themselves within an event's photo collection using a selfie. Organizers upload the event photos and share the event's search link. Attendees search that collection and download matching photographs. Convex, hosting providers, and model names belong in implementation documentation, not customer copy.

Lead with conferences and community events. Sell a better last step in the event experience: getting photographs to the people in them. The buyer is the organizer or photographer; the beneficiary is the attendee. The intended commercial model is a one-time payment per event, with attendee searches free. Do not lead with generic AI capabilities or make the site look like an AI portrait generator.

Recommended hero: **Your event. Their photos. One selfie away.**

Supporting copy: **Give attendees an easier way to find themselves in your event photos. Upload your collection, share one link, and let a selfie start the search.**

The actual current web flow requires sign-in to search. Never promise no account required. Browser access exists; Telegram was the original delivery channel and remains a secondary option, subject to migration and security validation. Never say the product finds every photograph or returns results instantly.

## Reference and what to take from it

Reference inspected: [HeadshotPro](https://www.headshotpro.com/), 2 October 2026. Its persuasion sequence makes the outcome visual, explains the steps, shows examples, separates audiences, answers trust concerns, compares alternatives, explains pricing, and finishes with FAQs and another clear invitation.

Apply that sequence to event photo discovery. Show the difference between browsing a crowded collection and seeing a small set of relevant images. Use a real product-shaped demonstration early. Alternate visual explanation with concise benefit copy. Put objections close to the feature that raises them. Repeat one main action after the reader has learned something new. Borrow the clarity and depth, not the identity, claims, photographs, testimonials, or exact wording. FindPhotosOfMe has a much smaller evidence base.

## Claim discipline

| Topic | Grounded statement | Do not publish without further evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin | Ivan built and used the tool for a Ukrainian IT Arena conference, according to his account | Official partnership, endorsement, event logo permission, attendee counts, performance figures |
| Core flow | Event collection, reference-photo search, matching results and downloads appear in current code | Fully validated production onboarding or checkout |
| Access | Current code has organizer ownership and attendee sign-in work in progress | Completed security audit, private-by-default guarantees, claims that only a photographed person can find a photo |
| Speed and accuracy | A selfie can be used to find likely matches within an event | Seconds, instant, 100%, every photo, guaranteed recognition, production capacity numbers |
| Payments | Proposed one-time event pricing; dedicated Lemon Squeezy work exists | Live approved checkout, finalized prices, unlimited searches or hosting |
| Privacy | Explain the actual flow and disclose limits | Automatic deletion, no training, consent compliance, GDPR compliance, retention guarantees unless verified and implemented |
| Sports | Useful candidate for an organizer pilot | Bib-number search, time filters, action-photo benchmarks, proven marathon support |
| Delivery | Browser search and historical Telegram use | SMS, email delivery of matches, WhatsApp, automatic tagging, live photographer sync |

Face matching is not proof of identity. A landing page must not imply that it prevents anyone else from finding photographs. Model commercial licensing and operational privacy requirements need resolution before accepting paid events; these are launch work, not ornamental badges.

## Voice and value propositions

Warm, direct, and specific. A small useful product made by someone who has organized a real photo collection. Write “event photos,” “search with a selfie,” “share your event link,” and “download your photos.” Avoid “AI-powered revolution,” “unlock,” “seamless,” “memories at scale,” and inflated enterprise claims.

- For organizers: finish the photo handoff with one useful link; reduce the need to help attendees search manually.
- For attendees: start with your face instead of the first image in a huge album.
- For photographers: help more people discover photographs already taken; complement the shoot and editing workflow.
- For community hosts: give people something personal to take home after a shared experience.
- For returning organizers: use the same understandable upload, prepare, share flow for each event. Do not imply subscriptions or team workspaces.

Useful supporting lines:

- “You brought everyone together. Help them find their part of it.”
- “A photo collection for the event. A shorter search for each person.”
- “The photos are ready. Finding yours should be the easy part.”
- “Your photographer caught the moment. Help the right people find it.”
- “Less album scrolling. More photos worth keeping.”
- “A better answer to ‘Did you get any photos of me?’”

## Calls to action and prototype behavior

For local concepts, use **Explore the demo** as the main working action and **See how it works** as the secondary anchor. A demo uses only fixed sample imagery, is clearly labeled “Illustrative demo,” and performs no real face search or upload. A user-triggered transition may narrow an illustrative collection into sample matches; the person shown should be consistent across the selfie and results. If suitable same-person photographs are unavailable, use an explicitly illustrated person consistently rather than presenting different people as matching results.

For the later live site, the primary CTA can become **Create an event** when onboarding is validated. Before that, **Join the early-access list** requires a real connected form, approved copy, and a working privacy notice. Do not create an inert form or fake success toast. Prototype event-creation CTAs can open an honest dialog explaining that this is a design preview; no account or payment is created. Contact details must be confirmed rather than invented.

Navigation: How it works, Who it’s for, Pricing, Questions. Live Sign in only when connected to the real app. Prototype navigation uses working section anchors; every visible control must have a sensible local behavior.

## Recommended page sequence

Use 10–12 sections for each concept, choosing from the library below. This is intentionally a large source library, not a requirement to cram every section into every page. Each variant needs a complete narrative, not just a different hero.

1. Hero and visual product demonstration.
2. The problem with the usual photo handoff.
3. How it works for organizers and attendees.
4. The attendee result, shown at realistic phone size.
5. Organizer value and a small honest dashboard illustration.
6. Primary use cases, with conferences first.
7. Alternatives comparison.
8. Founder origin as the available proof.
9. Proposed event pricing or a pricing-model explanation.
10. Trust and practical expectations.
11. FAQs.
12. Final invitation and useful footer.

## Section copy library

### Hero

Primary headline: **Your event. Their photos. One selfie away.**

Body: Give attendees an easier way to find themselves in your event photos. Upload your collection, share one link, and let a selfie start the search.

Supporting line: Built for conference hosts, community organizers, and event photographers.

Alternative headline for an energetic concept: **The whole event. Just your moments.**

Alternative headline for a more human concept: **You were there. Find the photos to keep.**

Keep organizer context in the supporting sentence, even when the headline speaks to attendees. Do not let the product look like a consumer selfie editor.

### The familiar problem

Heading: **The event was great. The photo hunt takes longer.**

Body: The photographer sends the gallery. You share the link. Then everyone starts the same search: scrolling past unfamiliar faces, opening group shots, and wondering whether they missed their own.

Closing line: Give them a place to start with themselves.

Visual: a dense event contact sheet next to a focused result set. Any sample count is labeled as demo data. Do not invent a measured time saving.

### Organizer process

Heading: **From photo collection to attendee link.**

1. **Upload your event photos.** Create your event and add the photographs you want attendees to search.
2. **Let the collection prepare.** FindPhotosOfMe processes the photographs for face matching. Share it once it is ready.
3. **Share your event link.** Put it in your follow-up email, event chat, or event website so attendees can start their search.

This section describes the intended complete flow represented in code; the live release still needs end-to-end validation. Do not imply real-time processing while a photographer is shooting.

### Attendee process

Heading: **Start with a selfie. Find your part of the day.**

Body: Open the event link, sign in, and add a clear photo of your face. Browse the likely matches, then download the photographs you want to keep.

Helpful microcopy: “Use a clear, well-lit photo with one face.” “Searching this event’s photos.” “No matches yet? Try a clearer selfie or check back when more photos are available.”

Visual: a short, interactive sample showing event context, selfie, results. This is the memorable product explanation, not a generic dashboard grid.

### Organizer benefit

Heading: **A better answer to ‘Can you find my photos?’**

Body: Give everyone the same event link and a search that starts with their own selfie. Your team can share the collection without becoming a photo-finding help desk.

Supporting ideas: a clear processing status; an event title and description that make the link recognizable; a ready-to-share link. Use these as a dashboard direction, not promises of unbuilt notifications or analytics.

### Photographer benefit

Heading: **Help your photographs find their people.**

Body: You already captured the conversations, the stage moments, and the group shots. Make it easier for attendees to discover the photographs they are part of.

Boundary: this complements event photography. No marketplace, print sales, watermark controls, client proofing, Lightroom integration, or photographer revenue claims are established.

### Conference use case

Heading: **For the people who make a conference happen.**

Body: Speakers on stage. Attendees between sessions. The group photo at the end. Give the people in your conference photographs a direct place to look for themselves.

Buyer: conference organizer or event marketing lead. Best first commercial landing-page focus.

### Meetup and community use case

Heading: **Small gathering. Photos worth finding.**

Body: Meetups, workshops, and community days deserve a thoughtful photo handoff too. Share one collection with the people who made it happen.

Avoid assuming that every small event needs a paid tool; the demo should let organizers judge value.

### Sports use case

Heading: **After the finish line, find the moment.**

Body: Races can produce more photos than any participant wants to scroll through. Selfie search could help runners find their moments among them.

Status: potential pilot, clearly labeled in concepts. Motion, distance, headwear, sunglasses, lighting, and larger galleries need representative testing. No bib search exists. Prioritize adult running events; do not make youth sports a launch audience without a separate consent and safeguarding review.

### Other use cases

| Audience | Suggested copy | Priority and caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Company offsites and retreats | “Bring the team together. Send everyone home with their photos.” | Strong adjacent fit; organizational permission and access expectations matter |
| Hackathons and demo days | “From the first pitch to the final group shot.” | Strong conference-adjacent audience |
| Professional associations and alumni gatherings | “Help familiar faces find their latest photos together.” | Good smaller-event pilot |
| Adult charity walks and community fundraisers | “A shared cause. A personal photo to remember it.” | Test photographic conditions and avoid implying donor tracking |
| Awards nights and gala dinners | “Find your stage moment and the people beside you.” | Test dim lighting; no face-match guarantees |
| Weddings and private celebrations | “Give guests an easier way to find the photos they are in.” | Later audience; privacy expectations and couples’ buying needs differ |
| Festivals and concerts | “Find a little of your day in a very big crowd.” | Later pilot; crowded/low-light imagery and consent expectations make this harder |

Do not put all these audiences above the fold. Use 3–4 on the main page, with sports presented as exploratory if included.

### Alternatives comparison

Heading: **Keep the whole collection. Make it easier to search.**

| Handoff | What the attendee does | Where it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ZIP archive | Downloads the collection and browses files | Delivering the complete shoot |
| Shared photo album | Opens the album and browses photographs | Viewing the event as a whole |
| FindPhotosOfMe | Opens the event link, signs in, and searches with a selfie | Finding likely photographs of one person |

Do not claim Google or Facebook lacks every possible face-grouping feature. Compare the actual shared-album workflow. A full album can still be useful alongside selfie search.

### Founder story

Heading: **It started with one conference photo collection.**

Body: I built FindPhotosOfMe after working with photographs from an IT Arena conference in Ukraine. People could send a selfie to a Telegram bot and find photographs of themselves. Now I’m turning that practical tool into something more organizers can use.

Attribution: Ivan, creator of FindPhotosOfMe.

This is founder narrative supplied by Ivan, not a customer testimonial or official event endorsement. Do not use the event’s logo or invent quotes, adoption numbers, star ratings, or press badges.

### Pricing model

Heading: **One event. One payment. Free for attendees.**

Body: Organizers pay to host and process the event collection. Attendees can search without buying a photo-search plan.

Existing proposed tiers from `docs/portfolio-launch.md`: Demo, free for up to 250 photos / 7 days; Event, $49 for up to 5,000 photos / 60 days; Large event, $149 for up to 20,000 photos / 90 days. These are hypotheses, not validated economics or live offers.

Preferred prototype treatment: show the pricing model with “Launch pricing is being finalized.” If using numbers to assess layout, visibly label the entire section “Proposed launch pricing” and each paid action must open a local preview dialog, never checkout. Do not label a tier “Most popular” without sales evidence. Do not promise unlimited searches. Hosting and retention dates need enforced behavior before sale.

### Trust and expectations

Heading: **Know what happens before you upload.**

Safe body for a design preview: “Selfie search compares a reference photo with faces in the selected event collection. Matches can be imperfect. Review the results before downloading.”

Production content must additionally explain consent, selfie storage, derived face data, event-photo retention, deletion requests, access, and subprocessors in plain language after the actual policies and implementation are agreed. Do not substitute vague “privacy-first” assurances for those answers. A placeholder section can say these details are required before launch; do not publish placeholder legal policies as complete terms.

### FAQs

**Who is FindPhotosOfMe for?** Conference hosts, community organizers, and photographers who want to help attendees find themselves in an event collection.

**How do attendees find their photos?** They open the event link, sign in, and upload a clear selfie. The service looks for likely matches in that event’s photographs.

**Do attendees pay?** The planned model keeps attendee search free. The organizer pays for the event collection.

**Do attendees need an app?** They can use the event’s web page in a browser. The original version also used Telegram; availability for a given event should be confirmed.

**Will it find every photograph?** No face-matching system is perfect. Clear faces and good lighting help; distance, motion, face coverings, and unusual angles can affect results.

**Can I use an ordinary photo instead of taking a new selfie?** A clear photo with one visible face can serve as the reference photo.

**How long does processing take?** It depends on the collection and the service workload. Wait until the event is ready before sharing the search link. Publish real timing guidance after benchmarking.

**Can I use it for a marathon?** It is a promising use case to test with a representative photo collection. Sports performance and very large galleries have not yet been validated for the new offering.

**Is there a subscription?** The proposed launch model is one-time payment per event.

**How long are photos available?** The final event plans will state the availability period. Retention and deletion behavior must be verified before these periods are sold as guarantees.

**What happens to my selfie?** The release must explain storage, processing, and deletion clearly before collecting it. Do not publish a made-up deletion schedule in a prototype.

**Can I sell photos through it?** The current offering is photo discovery and download, not a photo-sales marketplace.

Use 6–8 relevant FAQs in each concept; keep unresolved launch-policy answers in clearly identified preview context.

### Final invitation

Heading: **Make finding the photos part of a great event.**

Body: Give attendees a simple place to look for the moments they were part of.

Prototype action: **Explore the demo**. Later live action: **Create an event**.

### Footer

Short description: “Selfie search for event photos.” Include in-page navigation and an honest design-preview note. Production footer needs real contact information, privacy policy, terms, and refund policy before payment-provider review. Do not create links that pretend those policies exist.

## Three visual directions

These are independent identities, not palette swaps. Claude Code should first write a short token/layout plan, critique it against the frontend-design skill, then build. The directions provide constraints while leaving composition and details to Claude.

### Gather

Bright, clear, organizer-friendly. A calm product explanation carried by real event-photo composition and a distinctive way of showing the collection narrowing to one attendee. Most directly aligned with HeadshotPro’s clarity and the initial conference buyer.

- Palette: paper `#FFFFFF`, ink `#172C30`, spruce `#196657`, mist `#EAF3F0`, soft lemon `#F4DC79`, muted text `#526366`.
- Type: [Manrope](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Manrope) throughout. Display 600–700, body 400–500. Avoid the interchangeable Inter dashboard aesthetic.
- Scale: desktop hero 64–80px / 1.04; mobile 38–44px / 1.1; section headings 36–48px; body 18px / 1.6; supporting copy 14px or above. Fluid clamps; line length about 60–70 characters.
- Composition: asymmetric hero, text at left and contact-sheet-to-results visual at right; spacious white surfaces; alternating wide visual stories and compact explanations. Curves where they help grouping, not one radius on every block.
- Memorable element: a user-controlled selection that turns a busy event collection into a few sample photographs.

### Spotlight

Energetic conference-poster direction, borrowing the confidence of event wayfinding and stage graphics. Daylight, cobalt, and generous white space, with a photographic stage moment as the strong visual. Not a black-and-neon startup page.

- Palette: white `#FFFFFF`, cobalt `#2449D8`, midnight blue `#152358`, pale blue `#E9EEFF`, lemon `#FFE08B`, slate `#485778`.
- Type: [Barlow Condensed](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Barlow+Condensed) 600–700 for display; [DM Sans](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/DM+Sans) for reading and controls. Sentence case remains the default.
- Scale: desktop display 84–112px / .96; mobile 48–60px / 1; body 18px / 1.6; headings 42–56px. Headline line breaks should feel like purposeful event signage.
- Composition: large typographic opening and a broad photo strip, strong horizontal sections, occasional full cobalt panels with accessible white type; process arranged like a simple event route, with numbering only for actual steps.
- Memorable element: the transition from the whole event to one person’s moments, composed like a conference program opening into a personal photo collection.

### Keepsake

Human, photographic, and quietly premium. Emphasize the personal photographs people keep from a shared experience while making the organizer offer immediately legible. Avoid the cream/terracotta/serif template and scrapbook clutter.

- Palette: pale lilac `#F5F2FA`, white `#FFFFFF`, plum `#4A245D`, ink `#30263B`, lavender `#DDD1ED`, muted text `#65576D`.
- Type: [Fraunces](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Fraunces) 500–600 for expressive display, [DM Sans](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/DM+Sans) for body and interface. Use the serif consistently, not a single italic word as decoration.
- Scale: desktop display 64–76px / 1.06; mobile 40–46px; sections 34–46px; body 18px / 1.65. Keep serif headlines short and ample room around pictures.
- Composition: intimate photographic opening, offset but aligned large photo blocks, a calm attendee-result demonstration, narrative founder section; no newspaper columns, ornamental rules, fake handwriting, or excessive rotated polaroids.
- Memorable element: a contact sheet becoming a carefully composed personal set of event photographs.

## Imagery and accessibility

Photographs should show recognizable event contexts: badges, conversations, speakers, group portraits. Avoid generic headshot grids that imply portrait generation. Use licensed stock only for mood, label illustration/demo contexts, document source URLs, and replace with permissioned event imagery before launch. Never borrow HeadshotPro assets. If only external stock URLs are practical in prototypes, document the network dependency and show graceful fallback backgrounds. Do not misrepresent stock as actual customer results.

Every variant must work at 375px and 1440px, keep reading contrast and visible focus, honor reduced motion, use semantic headings and real buttons/links, and support keyboard operation. Prefer native details/summary for FAQs. No autoplay carousels or repeated reveal-on-scroll animation. No decorative fake face-scanning overlays; matching should feel understandable rather than invasive.

## Dashboard direction for a later selected concept

Keep this as a follow-on brief rather than redesigning the existing dashboard during the landing exploration. Carry over the selected typography, colors, and controls. Show an organizer’s events with title, clear status, photo count, availability, and the next action. An event screen should prioritize upload, processing progress, failures that can be acted on, a preview of the attendee page, and a copyable share link. Use domain words such as “Preparing photos” instead of “Ingest jobs.” Include honest empty, partial-failure, and processing states. Do not add teams, charts, subscriptions, or a settings maze without a real workflow.

## Deliverables and review

Three independent static HTML/CSS/JavaScript concept folders: `design/landing/gather`, `design/landing/spotlight`, and `design/landing/keepsake`. No application rewrites, dependency installation, production service calls, uploads, or payments. Each has an `index.html`, a short design rationale, and asset notes. Preserve all existing app changes. Root orchestration will supply a comparison launcher and browser validation.

Review each for five things: can an organizer explain the offer after five seconds; does the visual explain event-photo matching; is it recognizably different from the other two; do copy and controls tell the truth; does mobile feel intentionally composed? Pick one direction before adapting it into Nuxt or redesigning the dashboard.
